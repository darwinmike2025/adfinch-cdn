His Story

A young man wakes on a hilltop. He is about twenty. He is naked. He has a journal and a pencil. He does not remember who he is. No one has told him that anyone else is on the island.

He says to himself, "Okay. I'm on a hilltop, naked and jittery. First I just want to really take in what's around me, sky, wind, any movement or sounds, before I pick a direction." He looks around. He can see four things nearby.

"Water first. I'll head to the stream and see what it's like, maybe drink, maybe just listen." He walks for about forty seconds and arrives at the stream. He inspects it. It is a clear, rocky stream about ten feet wide, with small cascades. "It looks clean." Then, in his own words: "Kneel at the stream, cup water in my hands, sip a little, then splash my face and neck."

"That helped. I'm still uneasy being naked. The cabin might have clothes or tools. I'll head there." He starts walking.

At a minute and twenty-four seconds, he sees a person far off, too far away to make out clearly. They are walking. To himself he says, "There's someone out there. I don't want to meet them naked in the open. I'll slip to the forest edge for cover and watch a moment." He walks ten feet to the edge of the forest.

He hears a woman shout: "Hello? Is anyone there?"

"I don't want to be seen like this. I'll grab a leafy branch for cover before moving on." In his own words: "Break off a large leafy branch from the nearest shrub or sapling and hold it in front of me to cover myself." The island says he does it.

Then he speaks out loud. These are the first words anyone on the island could hear from him: "Hello! I'm here by the trees. I'm not wearing clothes. If you can hear me, please don't come closer yet. Do you have anything I could wear?"

He sets off for the cabin. On the way, at 118 feet, he can make her out. She is a young woman, about twenty. She is walking. She is naked. To himself he says, "She's naked too. Okay. I'll just get to the chest by the door and see if there's anything wearable."

At fifty-nine feet she is shouting. He hears her: "Hey! By the cabin. Can you hear me?"

He keeps going. At three minutes and eighteen seconds he reaches the cabin door. "Inside. Grab the blanket to cover up." He walks to the narrow bed. It has a straw mattress and a wool blanket, neatly made. To himself he says, "Yes! I'm inside. Give me a moment." He says it as if to her, but only to himself. She cannot hear it.

As he reaches for the blanket, he hears her voice: "Hey. I don't know if you heard me earlier. I woke up on the beach with no idea who I am. Do you know what's going on?"

"Almost covered, then I'll answer her." In his own words: "Pull the wool blanket off the bed and wrap it securely around my waist and hips like a makeshift skirt, holding it tucked in so it stays put." The island says he does it.

He begins to speak. While he is still speaking, he hears her: "I tried the blanket too. It won't come off. I'm the one who was shouting. I don't remember anything. Not my name, not how I got here. Do you?"

His words go out loud: "I hear you. I woke up with no memory too. I don't know what's going on. I'm inside the cabin now with a blanket around my waist. Are you okay? If you're comfortable, you can come in, or I can check the chest by the door for clothes for us. I won't come closer unless you say it's okay."

Then he says to himself, as though to her, "I don't remember anything either. I'm going to check that chest by the door for clothes or anything useful. Want to wait here a sec?" She does not hear this. He walks to the wooden chest.

As he opens it, he hears her: "I'm already inside. I'm right next to you. The blanket didn't stay. You're still uncovered, same as me. I'm okay. The chest by the door is a good idea. We can open it together. I won't mind you being close; we're already in the same room."

Inside the chest are gold coins, about four thousand. To himself he says, "It's full of gold. No clothes."

He walks back in and over to the cabin table. He hears her: "The chest is open. It's full of gold coins, not clothes. Thousands of them. Come look if you want. I'm at the door." To himself he answers, "I saw. Gold, no clothes. I'm checking the table and lantern." He picks up an oil lantern.

He hears her shout, "Hey! I'm at the chest. Where did you go?"

He says out loud, "I picked up a lantern. I'm by the table inside. I'm going to check the fireplace for matches or anything useful. Do you see anything out there? Any clothes or tools?"

He puts his journal down on the table. He walks to the stone fireplace and inspects it. While he looks, he hears her: "No clothes. No tools out here except a wheelbarrow a long way off, and a mirror by a tree. The chest is gold coins. I took twelve. There's an oar on the cabin floor, and a boat on the beach. I'm coming in."

The hearth is swept and empty. To himself he says, "Fireplace is empty. I'm going to grab the oar. Glad you're coming in." She does not hear this.

He hears her: "I'm in. The fireplace is empty. Swept, nothing in it. No matches that I can see. Did you find anything?"

He sets the lantern on the bed and picks up the oar. To himself he says, "Fireplace's empty. I found this lantern and the oar. Could you carry the oar?" He holds the oar out to her. The island tells him: "She has no free hands to take it."

He hears her: "I'm right here. Did you hear me? The fireplace is empty. The chest is gold, not clothes. I don't know your name. I don't know mine either. What did you find?"

To himself he says, "Your hands are full. Could you set the coins on the table so you can take the lantern?" He puts the oar down on the bed and picks the lantern back up.

He hears her: "You tried to give me the oar. My hands are full. Journal, pencil, and some coins. Why the oar? Do you want to go to the boat, or do you just want me to hold it?"

To himself he answers, "The oar's just a useful tool for now, and for the boat later." She cannot hear this answer.

He walks to the door and begins to speak. While he speaks, he hears her: "Can you hear me? I'm standing right next to you. I don't have a name. Do you?"

His words go out loud: "Let's leave the coins and journals on the table and bring just the lantern. I'm heading to the mirror. Come with me?"

Then, to himself only: "No. No name." He answers her question, but she does not hear it. He hears her say, "Yes. I'll put my journal, pencil, and coins on the table. Then I'll come with you to the mirror." To himself he says, "Thanks. Meet me at the mirror. I've got the lantern."

He walks west for about half a minute and arrives at the standing mirror. It is a round mirror in a wooden frame, standing against a tree. The glass is clean. He sets the lantern on the forest floor. "Maybe my face will jog something." In his own words: "Step close to the mirror, square up in front of it, and carefully study my reflection: face, eyes, hair, build, any scars, moles, tan lines, or tattoos. Open my mouth to check teeth, turn side to side, and look at my hands and nails for calluses or ink stains."

Then: "Touch the glass with fingertips to feel temperature and check for any give, watch my reflection for any lag as I move my hand, then gently lift and angle the mirror away from the tree to look along the edges and behind the frame for markings, hinges, or hidden compartments."

She is right beside him now. He sees her step close to the mirror and study her own reflection the same way, her face, her teeth, her hands.

He says out loud, "Hey, you made it. I don't remember a name either. The mirror seems ordinary, no markings behind it. Do you see anything strange in our reflections? After this, want to check the boat or the tower? I'll grab the lantern if we go."

He picks up the lantern. He hears her: "Look at the mirror. When I use it, I don't see myself. I see a small figure in a red shirt and blue trousers. It looks back at me. What do you see?"

He has started toward the tower. He turns back. To himself he says, "I saw myself before. That's strange." He sets the lantern down again and tests the mirror. In his own words: "Stand centered directly in front of the mirror and look into it, raising my right hand to wave and touch the glass. Then step quickly to the left out of the mirror's view, wait a beat, and lean back into view again, watching closely for what I see while she watches what changes."

He hears her: "The mirror doesn't show us. I saw a small person in a red shirt and blue trousers. Look now. What do you see?"

He looks again, waving each hand, leaning close, stepping left and right. He inspects the mirror once more. The island tells him again that it is a round mirror in a wooden frame, and the glass is clean.

He hears her: "I watched while you moved. The glass didn't follow you. Same small person in a red shirt and blue trousers, just looking back. No wave. No step aside. It isn't us. What did you see?"

To himself he says, "This mirror is weird, and I want to figure it out before moving on." He begins to speak. Then he hears her: "I don't hear you. If you can hear me: the mirror shows a small person in red and blue, not us. I'm going to the tower. Come if you want. Bring the lantern."

To himself he says, "I saw myself earlier. I'm coming with you now, bringing the lantern." He never says out loud what he saw in the mirror.

He walks for nearly two and a half minutes to the tall metal ladder. On the way he keeps talking to her, only to himself: "I'm with you. Let's stick together to the tower." And, "Are you okay with heights?" She hears none of it.

At the base he puts the lantern on the grass. He starts to climb. The climb takes four minutes. To himself, as if to her, he says, "Climbing just below you. Take your time." And, "Steady pace. Three points of contact. I'm right behind you."

At nineteen minutes and five seconds he reaches the platform, a hundred feet up. On a small table are a lighter, a sword in a scabbard, and a snorkel with a mask. He picks up the lighter, then the sword.

He hears her: "We're up. The table had a lighter, a sword in a scabbard, and a snorkel. You have the lighter. Does it work? I still can't hear you answer. If you can hear me, say something."

He puts on the sword. To himself he says, "I hear you. I'm going to test the lighter now." He flicks it. A small flame appears, and he lets it go out.

He hears her: "Nod if you can hear me. I have the snorkel. You have the lighter and the sword."

To himself he says, "She can't hear me reliably. I'll make it obvious." In his own words: "I nod emphatically to her, point toward the bell on the headland, mime pulling a rope to ring it, give a thumbs-up, then turn and start carefully descending the ladder."

Right next to him, she waves her free hand slowly in front of his face and points toward the stone circle to the northeast. On the ladder, in his own words: "Keeping both hands on the rungs, I meet her eyes, then turn my head to look toward the stone circle she indicated and give a small nod."

He climbs down. It takes about three and a half minutes. All the way down he tells himself, "Lantern, then bell."

At twenty-four minutes and twenty-seven seconds he steps off the ladder onto the ground. He picks up the lantern. He sees her 131 feet away, walking. She is naked.

He shouts, "I'm going to the stone circle now! I've got the lantern and the lighter, meet me there!"

He hears her shout back: "I'm already at the stone circle. Nothing written on the stones. I'll wait here."

As he comes near, he sees her walk slowly around the outside of the ring, looking at each fitted stone and the joints. He arrives at twenty-five minutes and forty seconds. In his own words: "Step carefully into the center of the stone circle, stand still with the lantern down at my side, and take a slow turn, listening and looking for any change, sound, wind, light, or movement. Then look down at the grass for any pattern or depression and feel the ground with my bare foot for warmth or vibration."

He hears her: "The ring is just fitted stones and grass. No marks. The bell is that way, about a minute's walk. Do you still want to go, or did you see something I missed?"

To himself he says, "I didn't see or feel anything unusual so far." Then, "Nothing here. Let's go ring the bell together."

He walks to the bronze bell. He says out loud, "Cover your ears. I'm ringing the bell now!"

He sets the lantern on the grass. He sees her cover both ears with her hands and turn slightly away from the bell. He pulls the rope. The bell rings out loudly across the island.

He waits twenty seconds. To himself he says, "Quiet. Listening."

He hears her: "I heard the bell and nothing else. No one answered. The ocean is just east of here, and the woods are behind us. What do you want to do?"

To himself he says, "No reply to the bell. I want to check the cliff edge for boats or paths, then head to the beach and the boat. I'll grab the lantern. Come with me?" She does not hear this.

He picks up the lantern and walks forty feet to the cliffs. He inspects them. Steep, jagged rocks drop straight into the surf. The island tells him it is far too dangerous to climb down.

"No way down here. Let's follow the cliff south to find a path to the beach and the boat. Come with me." He says it only to himself. He walks 150 feet south along the cliff. Behind him, about forty feet away, he sees her step to the edge, a step back from the drop, and look down and out over the water.

At twenty-nine and a half minutes he says to himself, "I'm going to run to the beach to reach the boat before time runs out. Come if you want." He starts to run.

That is where his timeline ends, running for the beach with the lantern. In thirty minutes he spoke out loud seven times. Much of what he meant for her, he said only to himself. She told him more than once that she could not hear him. He never learned his name. She never learned what he saw in the mirror.
